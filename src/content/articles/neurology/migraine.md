## Overview

**Migraine** is a common, inherited primary headache disorder affecting roughly 15% of the population — and three times as many women as men. It typically begins in adolescence or young adulthood, peaks in middle life, and improves with age. Migraine is far more than "just a headache": it is a complex neurological disorder with sensory, autonomic, cognitive and gastrointestinal features, and it ranks among the leading causes of years lived with disability worldwide.

Attacks last from 4 hours to 3 days and are characterised by moderate-to-severe unilateral, pulsating head pain with nausea and sensitivity to light and sound. About a third of patients experience an **aura** — a transient neurological symptom, usually visual — immediately before the headache.

## Pathophysiology

Migraine involves a complex interplay of neuronal and vascular mechanisms. Current understanding centres on **cortical spreading depression** — a wave of neural excitation followed by depression that moves across the cortex — which is thought to underlie aura and to activate the trigeminovascular system. Release of calcitonin gene-related peptide (CGRP) plays a key role in pain generation, and the recent development of CGRP-targeted therapies has transformed treatment.

Migraine has a strong genetic component. Specific monogenic subtypes exist (e.g. familial hemiplegic migraine), but common migraine is polygenic. Triggers are highly individual and include stress (and the post-stress let-down), hormonal fluctuations, sleep disruption, dehydration, skipped meals, bright or flickering light, certain foods and wine.

## Clinical features

The International Classification of Headache Disorders (ICHD-3) defines migraine without aura as at least five attacks with:

- Headache lasting 4–72 hours
- At least two of: unilateral, pulsating, moderate or severe, aggravated by routine activity
- At least one of: nausea/vomiting, photophobia and phonophobia

Migraine with aura requires at least two attacks with reversible neurological symptoms developing gradually over 5–60 minutes. Visual auras — scintillating scotomata, fortification spectra, or visual field loss — are most common. Sensory, motor (hemiplegic), brainstem and speech auras also occur.

A useful feature distinguishing migraine from tension-type headache is the need to seek a dark, quiet environment during an attack, with avoidance of movement.

## Differential diagnosis

Migraine must be distinguished from:

- **Tension-type headache** — bilateral, pressing, mild-to-moderate, no nausea
- **Cluster headache** — severe unilateral orbital pain in clusters, with autonomic features
- **Secondary headache** — raised intracranial pressure, [stroke](/articles/neurology/stroke), giant cell arteritis, meningitis

Red-flag features prompting neuroimaging include sudden-onset ("thunderclap") headache, new headache in older age, focal neurological signs, systemic features, or change in an established pattern.

## Acute management

Acute treatment aims to terminate an attack and restore function. Options include:

- **Simple analgesics** — paracetamol, NSAIDs (ibuprofen, naproxen) — first line for mild-to-moderate attacks
- **Triptans** — 5-HT₁B/₁D agonists (sumatriptan, rizatriptan, eletriptan) — for moderate-to-severe attacks; most effective when taken early
- **Anti-emetics** — metoclopramide or prochlorperazine, useful for nausea and as adjuvants
- **Gepants** — small-molecule CGRP receptor antagonists (ubrogepant, rimegepant) — useful when triptans are contraindicated
- **Ditans** — lasmiditan, a 5-HT₁F agonist

Triptans are contraindicated in coronary artery disease, uncontrolled [hypertension](/articles/cardiology/hypertension), and previous [myocardial infarction](/articles/cardiology/myocardial-infarction), because of their vasoconstrictive effect.

## Preventive management

Preventive therapy is considered when attacks occur more than four times monthly, are severely disabling, or fail to respond to acute therapy. Options include:

- **Beta-blockers** — propranolol, metoprolol
- **Anticonvulsants** — topiramate, valproate
- **Tricyclics** — amitriptyline
- **CGRP monoclonal antibodies** — erenumab, fremanezumab, galcanezumab — well tolerated, game-changing for many patients
- **OnabotulinumtoxinA** — injected for chronic migraine (≥ 15 headache days/month)

Lifestyle measures — regular sleep, meals and hydration, stress management, and avoidance of identified triggers — are foundational and reduce attack frequency.

## Prognosis

Migraine is a chronic condition with a fluctuating course. Many patients improve with age, particularly after menopause in women. A subset progresses to **chronic migraine** (≥ 15 headache days/month for more than three months), which is more disabling and harder to treat. With appropriate acute and preventive therapy, most patients can achieve meaningful reduction in attack frequency and return to normal function.
