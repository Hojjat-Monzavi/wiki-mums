## Overview

A **stroke** is the sudden onset of a focal neurological deficit caused by disruption of cerebral blood flow. It is the second leading cause of death worldwide and the leading cause of acquired adult disability. Stroke is a time-critical emergency — "time is brain" — because neurons irreversibly die within minutes of ischaemia.

Strokes are divided into two broad types:

- **Ischaemic stroke** — occlusion of a cerebral artery by thrombus or embolus (≈ 85% of cases)
- **Haemorrhagic stroke** — rupture of a cerebral vessel, including intracerebral and subarachnoid haemorrhage (≈ 15%)

The two are clinically indistinguishable in many cases and require urgent brain imaging to differentiate, because their treatments are diametrically opposite.

## Pathophysiology

The brain consumes roughly 20% of the body's oxygen despite being only 2% of its weight, and it has minimal energy reserves. Cerebral blood flow below about 20 mL/100 g/min produces a functionally silent **penumbra** — tissue that is electrically quiet but still viable. Restoring flow before the penumbra infarcts is the rationale for thrombolysis and thrombectomy.

Major risk factors overlap with those for cardiovascular disease: [hypertension](/articles/cardiology/hypertension), [atrial fibrillation](/articles/cardiology/arrhythmia), diabetes, smoking, dyslipidaemia, obesity, and previous TIA or stroke. Atrial fibrillation increases stroke risk approximately five-fold.

## Clinical features

Stroke should be suspected with any **sudden** focal neurological deficit. The FAST campaign teaches the public to recognise:

- **F**ace — unilateral facial weakness
- **A**rm — unilateral limb weakness
- **S**peech — slurred or absent speech
- **T**ime — to call emergency services

Clinicians use the NIH Stroke Scale (NIHSS) to quantify severity. Specific syndromes suggest the vessel involved: contralateral hemiparesis and hemisensory loss with facial sparing suggests lacunar stroke; hemianopia and hemineglect suggests posterior circulation; pure hemianopia suggests occipital cortex involvement.

Haemorrhagic stroke may present with severe headache, vomiting, and depressed consciousness — particularly subarachnoid haemorrhage, classically described as a "thunderclap" headache — the worst headache of the patient's life.

## Diagnosis

Immediate **non-contrast CT** of the brain is the first-line investigation. It reliably identifies haemorrhage (which appears bright white) within minutes, but may be normal in early ischaemia. CT or MR angiography identifies large-vessel occlusion amenable to mechanical thrombectomy.

Subsequent work-up seeks the underlying cause:

- ECG and prolonged cardiac monitoring for paroxysmal atrial fibrillation
- Carotid duplex for symptomatic carotid stenosis
- Echocardiography if cardioembolic source suspected
- Blood glucose, lipids, HbA1c, clotting screen

## Management

### Acute
- **Airway, breathing, circulation** — oxygen only if hypoxic
- **Blood pressure management** — permissive hypertension to maintain cerebral perfusion in ischaemic stroke (target < 185/110 mmHg if thrombolysis planned); aggressive lowering in haemorrhagic stroke
- **IV thrombolysis** (alteplase or tenecteplase) within 4.5 hours of onset for ischaemic stroke, after excluding haemorrhage
- **Mechanical thrombectomy** for anterior circulation large-vessel occlusion within 6 hours (up to 24 hours in selected patients with favourable imaging)
- **Neurosurgical evacuation** for cerebellar haemorrhage > 3 cm or hydrocephalus

### Secondary prevention
- Antiplatelet therapy (aspirin ± clopidogrel) for non-cardioembolic stroke
- Anticoagulation for cardioembolic stroke due to AF
- High-intensity statin
- Blood pressure reduction to < 130/80 mmHg
- Carotid endarterectomy for symptomatic severe stenosis
- Lifestyle: smoking cessation, exercise, dietary modification

## Complications

Stroke complications are a major source of morbidity and mortality:

- **Aspiration pneumonia** — from dysphagia; swallow assessment before oral intake is mandatory
- **Venous thromboembolism** — prophylactic low-molecular-weight heparin once haemorrhage excluded
- **Pressure sores, contractures, falls** — addressed by early mobilisation and physiotherapy
- **Post-stroke depression** — affects up to a third of patients; screen and treat
- **Seizures** — early (within 7 days) or late; recurrent seizures are termed [epilepsy](/articles/neurology/epilepsy)

## Prognosis

Outcome depends on stroke severity (NIHSS), age, comorbidities and time to reperfusion. Approximately one-third of stroke survivors return to independent function, one-third have significant residual disability, and one-third die. Early specialist care in a stroke unit significantly improves outcomes across all stroke types.
