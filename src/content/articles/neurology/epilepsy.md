## Overview

**Epilepsy** is a chronic neurological disorder defined by an enduring predisposition to generate **epileptic seizures** — transient neurological symptoms caused by excessive, hypersynchronous neuronal discharges. It is one of the most common serious neurological conditions, affecting about 1% of the population worldwide, and carries significant social stigma in many cultures despite being treatable in the majority of patients.

The International League Against Epilepsy defines epilepsy as any of:

- At least two unprovoked seizures > 24 hours apart
- One unprovoked seizure with a ≥ 60% probability of further seizures over 10 years
- Diagnosis of an epilepsy syndrome

## Classification

Seizures are first classified by their onset:

### Focal (partial) seizures
Originate within one hemisphere. May occur with preserved awareness, with impaired awareness, or with progression to a **focal-to-bilateral tonic-clonic** seizure. Symptoms depend on the cortex involved — motor (jerking), sensory (tingling, smells), autonomic, or psychic (déjà vu, fear).

### Generalised seizures
Originate rapidly in both hemispheres. Subtypes include:

- **Absence** — brief staring, often in children, with 3 Hz spike-wave on EEG
- **Tonic-clonic** — loss of consciousness with stiffening then jerking of all limbs
- **Myoclonic** — sudden brief muscle jerks
- **Atonic** — sudden loss of muscle tone ("drop attacks")

## Aetiology

Epilepsy has six broad aetiological categories:

1. **Structural** — tumour, malformation of cortical development, mesial temporal sclerosis, traumatic brain injury, [stroke](/articles/neurology/stroke) scar
2. **Genetic** — inherited channelopathies, SCN1A (Dravet syndrome), GABA-A receptor mutations
3. **Infectious** — neurocysticercosis, viral encephalitis, bacterial meningitis
4. **Metabolic** — hypoglycaemia, hyponatraemia, hepatic encephalopathy
5. **Immune** — anti-NMDA receptor encephalitis, lupus cerebritis
6. **Unknown** — approximately half of cases

## Clinical features

A detailed **witness account** is invaluable because patients are typically amnesic for the event itself. Useful features include:

- **Aura** — a subjective sensation at seizure onset (rising epigastric feeling, odd smell, déjà vu)
- **Motor features** — stiffening, jerking, lateral tongue biting, urinary incontinence
- **Duration** — most seizures last 1–3 minutes; status epilepticus is ≥ 5 minutes
- **Postictal state** — confusion, drowsiness, headache, Todd's paralysis (transient focal weakness)

A generalised tonic-clonic seizure typically produces 1–2 minutes of unconsciousness with rhythmic jerking, followed by 10–30 minutes of postictal confusion.

## Diagnosis

Diagnosis combines history, EEG and neuroimaging:

- **EEG** — interictal epileptiform discharges support the diagnosis; ictal EEG is diagnostic but rarely captured unless video-EEG telemetry is performed
- **MRI brain** — high-resolution, epilepsy-protocol — identifies structural lesions
- **Blood tests** — glucose, electrolytes, calcium, magnesium, toxins
- **Cardiac work-up** if syncope is in the differential — ECG, occasionally tilt-table testing

The history must also distinguish seizures from **non-epileptic attack disorder**, syncope, [migraine](/articles/neurology/migraine) with aura, and transient ischaemic attack — these mimics are commonly misdiagnosed as epilepsy.

## Management

### Acute (status epilepticus)
- First-line: intravenous lorazepam 4 mg (repeated once)
- Second-line: intravenous levetiracetam, fosphenytoin, or sodium valproate
- Third-line (refractory): intubation and infusion of midazolam, propofol or thiopentone in intensive care

### Long-term antiseizure medications
Choice depends on seizure type, syndrome, age, sex and comorbidities:

- **Focal seizures** — lamotrigine, levetiracetam, lacosamide
- **Generalised tonic-clonic** — sodium valproate (avoid in women of childbearing age), levetiracetam
- **Absence** — ethosuximide or sodium valproate
- **Myoclonic** — sodium valproate, levetiracetam

About two-thirds of patients achieve seizure freedom on the first or second monotherapy. The remaining third have **drug-resistant epilepsy** and should be considered for epilepsy surgery (curative in selected focal epilepsies), vagus nerve stimulation, or dietary therapy (ketogenic diet, particularly in children).

## Prognosis

The prognosis depends heavily on aetiology and syndrome. Idiopathic generalised epilepsies usually respond well to medication and may remit. Mesial temporal lobe epilepsy with hippocampal sclerosis often remains drug-resistant but is the best surgical candidate. Sudden Unexpected Death in Epilepsy (SUDEP) is a rare but important risk, particularly in uncontrolled tonic-clonic seizures.
