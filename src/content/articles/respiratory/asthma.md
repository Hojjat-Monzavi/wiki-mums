## Overview

**Asthma** is a chronic inflammatory disorder of the airways in which exposure to triggers produces bronchoconstriction, mucosal oedema and mucus production, leading to intermittent airflow obstruction. It affects approximately 300 million people worldwide and is the commonest chronic disease of childhood. The hallmark of asthma — distinguishing it from [COPD](/articles/respiratory/copd) — is that the airway obstruction is **reversible**, either spontaneously or with treatment.

Asthma runs in families and is closely linked to atopy — the tendency to develop allergic conditions such as eczema, allergic rhinitis and food allergy. Most cases begin in childhood, but adult-onset asthma also occurs and may be triggered by occupational exposures, hormones, or infections.

## Pathophysiology

Asthma involves both **type 2 (eosinophilic) inflammation** — driven by allergens, IL-4, IL-5 and IL-13 — and **non-type 2 inflammation**, which includes neutrophilic asthma and obesity-related asthma. Chronic inflammation produces airway hyperresponsiveness and, over time, structural changes called **airway remodelling** (sub-basement fibrosis, smooth muscle hypertrophy, goblet cell hyperplasia).

Common triggers include:

- Allergens — house dust mite, pollen, animal dander, mould
- Infections — particularly rhinovirus in children
- Exercise, cold air, and changes in weather
- Irritants — tobacco smoke, air pollution, fumes
- Drugs — non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), beta-blockers
- Stress and emotion

## Clinical features

The classic symptoms are:

- **Wheeze** — high-pitched whistling on expiration
- **Breathlessness**
- **Chest tightness**
- **Cough** — particularly nocturnal or early morning, often non-productive

Symptoms are typically variable, worse at night and early morning, and provoked by the triggers listed above. A personal or family history of atopy supports the diagnosis.

Examination may be normal between attacks. During an exacerbation there is expiratory wheeze, prolonged expiration, tachypnoea, tachycardia and use of accessory muscles. **Silent chest** — absence of wheeze with poor air entry — is a pre-terminal sign of severe bronchoconstriction and requires immediate intervention.

## Diagnosis

Asthma is a clinical diagnosis supported by objective airflow testing:

- **Spirometry** — FEV₁/FVC ratio < 0.7 with ≥ 12% and 200 mL reversibility after bronchodilator
- **Peak expiratory flow** — diurnal variability > 10% over 1–2 weeks
- **Fractional exhaled nitric oxide (FeNO)** — elevated in eosinophilic asthma (> 40 ppb in adults)
- **Bronchial provocation** with methacholine or histamine — for suspected asthma with normal spirometry

Differential diagnosis includes COPD (older smokers, fixed obstruction), vocal cord dysfunction, gastro-oesophageal reflux, cardiac failure ("cardiac asthma"), and in children, foreign body aspiration and cystic fibrosis.

## Management

Asthma treatment is **stepwise**, aiming for the lowest effective therapy:

1. **SABA** — short-acting beta-agonist (salbutamol) for symptom relief as needed
2. **Low-dose inhaled corticosteroid (ICS)** — beclometasone, budesonide — for patients using SABA more than twice weekly
3. **ICS + LABA** — long-acting beta-agonist (formoterol, salmeterol) — preferably as a combination inhaler
4. **Medium/high-dose ICS + LABA**, consider add-on **LAMA** (tiotropium)
5. **Add-on biologics** — anti-IgE (omalizumab), anti-IL-5 (mepolizumab, benralizumab), anti-IL-4Rα (dupilumab) for severe asthma

GINA now recommends **SMART** (Single Maintenance and Reliever Therapy) — using a combination ICS-formoterol inhaler both as maintenance and as reliever — as the preferred approach for most patients. This reduces severe exacerbations compared with SABA-only reliever strategies.

Every patient should have a written **asthma action plan** that specifies maintenance therapy, escalation steps, oral steroid initiation, and when to seek emergency care.

## Acute exacerbation

Acute exacerbations are medical emergencies. Initial management is:

- **Oxygen** to maintain SpO₂ 93–95%
- **Repeated inhaled salbutamol** via spacer or nebuliser
- **Ipratropium bromide** added for severe attacks
- **Systemic corticosteroids** — oral prednisolone 40–50 mg or intravenous hydrocortisone
- **Magnesium sulphate** IV for life-threatening exacerbation

Features of life-threatening asthma include SpO₂ < 92%, silent chest, cyanosis, exhaustion, and poor respiratory effort. Such patients need intensive care monitoring.

## Prognosis

Childhood asthma often improves in adolescence, although some children — particularly those with severe disease, atopy and reduced lung function — persist into adulthood. Adult asthma is usually a chronic condition requiring lifelong inhaled therapy. With appropriate preventer therapy, the vast majority of patients achieve good control and lead normal lives. Asthma mortality is rare with modern management but remains concentrated in patients with poorly controlled disease and recent severe exacerbations.
