## Overview

**Pneumonia** is an acute infection of the lung parenchyma. It is one of the commonest reasons for adult hospital admission and remains a leading cause of death worldwide, particularly at the extremes of age. Pneumonia is classified by where it was acquired, because this strongly predicts the causative organism:

- **Community-acquired pneumonia (CAP)** — acquired outside hospital
- **Hospital-acquired pneumonia (HAP)** — acquired ≥ 48 hours after admission
- **Ventilator-associated pneumonia (VAP)** — in intubated patients
- **Aspiration pneumonia** — following macro-aspiration of oropharyngeal or gastric contents

## Aetiology

The commonest cause of CAP is **Streptococcus pneumoniae** (pneumococcus). Other typical bacteria include Haemophilus influenzae and Moraxella catarrhalis. **Atypical** pathogens — Mycoplasma pneumoniae, Chlamydia pneumoniae, Legionella pneumophila — produce syndromes with prominent extrapulmonary features. Viruses (influenza, RSV, SARS-CoV-2) are increasingly recognised as primary pathogens. Staphylococcus aureus and Klebsiella pneumoniae cause severe cavitating pneumonia, particularly post-viral.

Risk factors include age > 65, chronic lung disease ([COPD](/articles/respiratory/copd), [asthma](/articles/respiratory/asthma)), heart failure, diabetes, immunosuppression, smoking, alcohol excess, and aspiration risk (stroke, dysphagia, reduced consciousness).

## Clinical features

The classical presentation of bacterial pneumonia is abrupt:

- **Fever**, often with rigors
- **Pleuritic chest pain** — sharp, worse on inspiration or cough
- **Productive cough** — purulent or rust-coloured sputum
- **Dyspnoea**

Atypical pneumonia often presents more insidiously with dry cough, fever, headache, myalgia and fatigue. In elderly or immunocompromised patients, confusion may be the only initial sign.

Examination typically reveals tachypnoea, tachycardia, dullness to percussion over consolidated lung, bronchial breath sounds, and crackles. Hypoxia (SpO₂ < 92%) is an important severity marker.

## Diagnosis

Pneumonia is a **clinical-radiological diagnosis**:

- **Chest X-ray** — required to confirm consolidation. Lobar consolidation suggests pneumococcus; bilateral patchy infiltrates suggest atypical or viral pneumonia; cavitation suggests S. aureus, Klebsiella or tuberculosis.
- **Blood tests** — full blood count (leukocytosis), CRP (markedly elevated), urea and electrolytes, liver function
- **Microbiology** — sputum Gram stain and culture, blood cultures (before antibiotics if possible), urine pneumococcal and Legionella antigens in severe cases, respiratory viral PCR
- **Arterial blood gas** — for severe disease or concern about hypercapnia

Severity is assessed with the **CURB-65** score: Confusion, Urea > 7 mmol/L, Respiratory rate ≥ 30, Blood pressure < 90/60, Age ≥ 65. Score ≥ 3 warrants hospital admission; ≥ 4 ICU consideration.

## Management

### Antibiotic therapy
Empirical therapy is guided by severity and setting:

- **Low severity (CAP, ambulatory)** — amoxicillin orally; doxycycline or clarithromycin if penicillin-allergic
- **Moderate severity (hospital, non-ICU)** — amoxicillin + macrolide IV, or respiratory fluoroquinolone
- **Severe (ICU)** — broad-spectrum beta-lactam (co-amoxiclav or ceftriaxone) + macrolide; add antiviral if influenza suspected

Therapy is narrowed once microbiology results return. Typical duration is 5–7 days for uncomplicated CAP, longer for Staphylococcus, Legionella, or complicated pneumonia.

### Supportive care
- **Oxygen** to maintain SpO₂ 94–98% (or 88–92% in COPD)
- **IV fluids** if dehydrated
- **Analgesia** for pleuritic pain
- **Venous thromboembolism prophylaxis** for hospitalised patients

### Complications
- **Parapneumonic effusion** — drain if large or infected (empyema)
- **Lung abscess** — prolonged antibiotics, sometimes surgical drainage
- **Septic shock** and **acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS)** in severe cases
- **Acute kidney injury**, **hyponatraemia** (SIADH)

## Prevention

- **Pneumococcal vaccination** — PCV20 in adults ≥ 65 or high-risk groups
- **Influenza vaccine** annually
- **COVID-19 vaccination**
- **Smoking cessation**
- **Oral hygiene** and aspiration precautions in patients with dysphagia

## Prognosis

Most ambulatory CAP recovers within 1–2 weeks of appropriate antibiotics, although fatigue and cough may persist for 6–8 weeks. Hospitalised CAP carries 5–10% in-hospital mortality. Mortality rises sharply with age, comorbidity, and severity scores. Pneumonia precipitating admission in patients with advanced [COPD](/articles/respiratory/copd) or heart failure has a particularly poor prognosis.
