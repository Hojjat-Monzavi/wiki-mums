## Overview

**Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD)** is a common, preventable and treatable disease characterised by persistent, usually progressive airflow limitation. It is caused by chronic inflammation of the airways and lung parenchyma, almost always from long-term exposure to noxious particles or gases — most importantly **tobacco smoke**. COPD is the third leading cause of death worldwide and is a major cause of chronic disability.

COPD encompasses two overlapping phenotypes: **chronic bronchitis** (chronic productive cough for at least three months in two consecutive years) and **emphysema** (destruction of alveolar parenchyma). The distinction has limited clinical use today because most patients have features of both.

## Pathophysiology

In susceptible individuals, inhaled toxins — chiefly cigarette smoke, but also biomass fuel in low-income countries — trigger chronic inflammation in the airways and alveoli. This produces:

- **Mucous gland hypertrophy** and goblet cell hyperplasia → chronic sputum
- **Small airway fibrosis** and narrowing → airflow limitation
- **Parenchymal destruction** (protease-antiprotease imbalance) → loss of elastic recoil, emphysema
- **Pulmonary vascular remodelling** → pulmonary hypertension and cor pulmonale

The airflow obstruction is **largely irreversible**, distinguishing COPD from [asthma](/articles/respiratory/asthma). However, there is often a small reversible component, and many patients have overlap features.

α₁-antitrypsin deficiency should be suspected in younger patients (< 45), non-smokers, or those with basal-predominant emphysema and a family history.

## Clinical features

COPD typically presents in middle-aged or older smokers with:

- **Progressive exertional dyspnoea** — the cardinal symptom
- **Chronic cough** — often worse in the morning
- **Sputum production** — mucoid, becoming purulent during exacerbations
- **Wheeze and chest tightness**

Examination may show prolonged expiration, pursed-lip breathing, barrel chest, hyper-resonance to percussion, and diminished breath sounds with rhonchi. In advanced disease there is cachexia, cyanosis, and signs of right heart failure (peripheral oedema, raised JVP) — **cor pulmonale**.

A productive cough changing in character — increased volume, purulence or breathlessness — defines an **acute exacerbation** and is most commonly triggered by viral or bacterial infection.

## Diagnosis

Diagnosis requires **post-bronchodilator spirometry** showing a fixed obstructive defect:

- **FEV₁/FVC < 0.70** (or below lower limit of normal)
- Severity graded by FEV₁ as % predicted: GOLD 1 (≥ 80%), 2 (50–79%), 3 (30–49%), 4 (< 30%)

Supporting investigations:

- **Chest X-ray** — hyperinflation, flat hemidiaphragms, bullae
- **CT chest** — characterises emphysema distribution, excludes alternative diagnoses
- **Alpha-1 antitrypsin level** in younger or non-smoking patients
- **Arterial blood gas** if SpO₂ < 92% or for assessment of hypercapnia
- **ECG and echocardiogram** — for pulmonary hypertension and cor pulmonale

## Management

COPD management is also stepwise and symptom-driven:

### Smoking cessation
The single most effective intervention. Slows the rate of FEV₁ decline to that of a non-smoker. Combine behavioural support with nicotine replacement therapy, varenicline or bupropion.

### Inhaled therapy
- **Short-acting bronchodilators** (SABA, SAMA) — for immediate symptom relief
- **LABA + LAMA** combinations — preferred first-line maintenance for symptomatic patients
- **Inhaled corticosteroids** — added for patients with exacerbations or eosinophilia; risk of pneumonia
- Newer class: **PDE4 inhibitors** (roflumilast) for severe chronic bronchitis

### Non-pharmacological
- **Pulmonary rehabilitation** — exercise training, education, behavioural change; improves exercise capacity and quality of life
- **Long-term oxygen therapy** — for patients with PaO₂ < 55 mmHg or < 60 mmHg with cor pulmonale; improves survival
- **Non-invasive ventilation** — for chronic hypercapnic respiratory failure
- **Vaccinations** — influenza annually, pneumococcal, COVID-19, pertussis
- **Lung volume reduction surgery or endobronchial valves** — for selected patients with upper-lobe emphysema

### Acute exacerbation
- **Oxygen** — controlled, target SpO₂ 88–92% to avoid hypercapnia
- **Bronchodilators** — nebulised salbutamol and ipratropium
- **Systemic corticosteroids** — prednisolone 30–40 mg for 5–7 days
- **Antibiotics** — for purulent sputum; first-line amoxicillin or doxycycline
- **Non-invasive ventilation** — for acidotic (pH < 7.35) hypercapnic respiratory failure

## Prognosis

COPD is progressive but highly variable. Median survival from diagnosis is roughly 8–10 years, heavily dependent on smoking status, exacerbation frequency, and comorbidities (cardiovascular disease, lung cancer, osteoporosis, depression). The BODE index — Body mass index, Obstruction, Dyspnoea, Exercise capacity — predicts mortality better than FEV₁ alone.
