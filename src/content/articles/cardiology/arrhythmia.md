## Overview

An **arrhythmia** is any disturbance of the heart's normal rhythm. The heart's electrical system normally originates each heartbeat in the sinoatrial node, conducts it through the atria to the atrioventricular node, and then distributes it through the His-Purkinje fibres to the ventricles. Disruption anywhere along this path produces an arrhythmia.

Arrhythmias are traditionally grouped by rate and origin:

- **Tachyarrhythmias** — heart rate > 100 bpm
- **Bradyarrhythmias** — heart rate < 60 bpm
- **Ectopic beats** — premature atrial or ventricular complexes

The most clinically important sustained arrhythmia is **atrial fibrillation**, the commonest sustained cardiac rhythm disturbance and a major cause of [stroke](/articles/neurology/stroke).

## Atrial fibrillation

Atrial fibrillation (AF) is characterised by chaotic, disorganised atrial electrical activity with an irregularly irregular ventricular response. Prevalence rises sharply with age and it is strongly associated with [hypertension](/articles/cardiology/hypertension), heart failure, obesity, sleep apnoea and excess alcohol. AF may be paroxysmal (self-terminating), persistent (sustained but amenable to cardioversion) or permanent.

Management has two parallel goals: **rate or rhythm control** and **stroke prevention**. Rate control (typically with a beta-blocker or non-dihydropyridine calcium-channel blocker) is the initial strategy for most patients. Rhythm control — antiarrhythmic drugs or catheter ablation — is appropriate for symptomatic patients or those with recent-onset AF.

Stroke risk is estimated with the **CHA₂DS₂-VASc score** (Congestive heart failure, Hypertension, Age ≥ 75 (doubled), Diabetes, Stroke/TIA (doubled), Vascular disease, Age 65–74, Sex category). Anticoagulation with a direct oral anticoagulant (apixaban, rivaroxaban, dabigatran) or warfarin is recommended for most patients with a score of ≥ 2 in men or ≥ 3 in women.

## Other common arrhythmias

### Supraventricular tachycardia (SVT)
A regular narrow-complex tachycardia, often arising from a re-entrant circuit involving the AV node. Episodes cause abrupt-onset palpitations. Acute termination is with vagal manoeuvres, then intravenous adenosine. Catheter ablation is curative for recurrent SVT.

### Ventricular tachycardia (VT)
A wide-complex tachycardia originating in the ventricles. Sustained VT is a medical emergency — it may cause syncope or degenerate into ventricular fibrillation. Management is immediate defibrillation if the patient is unstable, otherwise intravenous amiodarone. Implantable cardioverter-defibrillators (ICDs) are indicated for secondary prevention.

### Bradycardia
Defined as a sustained heart rate below 60 bpm. Causes include sinus node dysfunction ("sick sinus syndrome"), AV block, and drugs (beta-blockers, non-dihydropyridine calcium-channel blockers, digoxin). Symptomatic bradycardia unresponsive to drug withdrawal is treated with a permanent pacemaker.

## Clinical features

Patients with arrhythmia may describe:

- **Palpitations** — awareness of an irregular, fast, or pounding heartbeat
- **Dizziness** or presyncope
- **Syncope** — particularly with VT or high-grade AV block
- **Dyspnoea** or chest pain
- **Fatigue** — especially in chronic AF with poor rate control

A description of the rhythm — sudden vs gradual onset, regular vs irregular, fast vs slow — helps narrow the differential before an ECG is obtained.

## Diagnosis

A **12-lead ECG** is the single most useful investigation and should be obtained during symptoms if at all possible. If symptoms are intermittent, ambulatory ECG monitoring (24-hour Holter, event monitor, or implantable loop recorder) captures the rhythm during an episode. Blood tests exclude electrolyte disturbance, thyroid dysfunction, and ischaemia. Echocardiography identifies structural heart disease.

## Management

Management is dictated by the rhythm:

- **Stable AF**: rate control ± anticoagulation
- **Unstable tachyarrhythmia**: synchronised DC cardioversion
- **VT/VF**: defibrillation plus advanced life support
- **Symptomatic bradycardia**: atropine acutely, then permanent pacemaker
- **Re-entrant SVT**: vagal manoeuvres, adenosine, ablation

All patients should have reversible triggers addressed — ischaemia, electrolyte disturbance, thyrotoxicosis, alcohol excess, sleep apnoea.

## Prognosis

The prognosis is highly rhythm-dependent. Benign ectopy in a structurally normal heart carries an excellent prognosis. Sustained VT in heart failure carries a high annual mortality. AF doubles mortality overall and increases stroke risk five-fold without anticoagulation.
