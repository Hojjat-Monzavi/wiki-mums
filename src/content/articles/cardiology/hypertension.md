## Overview

**Hypertension** is the medical term for persistently elevated arterial blood pressure. It is one of the most common chronic conditions worldwide and a major modifiable risk factor for [myocardial infarction](/articles/cardiology/myocardial-infarction), [stroke](/articles/neurology/stroke), heart failure, chronic kidney disease and premature death. Most patients have no symptoms for many years, which is why hypertension is sometimes called the "silent killer."

Blood pressure is recorded as two numbers in millimetres of mercury (mmHg): systolic (during ventricular contraction) over diastolic (during ventricular relaxation). Modern guidelines define hypertension as a sustained office reading of ≥130/80 mmHg (ACC/AHA) or ≥140/90 mmHg (NICE/ESC), with elevations confirmed by ambulatory or home monitoring rather than a single visit.

## Classification

| Category | Systolic (mmHg) | Diastolic (mmHg) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Normal | < 120 | < 80 |
| Elevated | 120–129 | < 80 |
| Stage 1 hypertension | 130–139 | 80–89 |
| Stage 2 hypertension | ≥ 140 | ≥ 90 |

About 90–95% of cases are **primary (essential)** hypertension, in which no single identifiable cause is found. The remainder are **secondary hypertension**, due to conditions such as renal artery stenosis, primary hyperaldosteronism, pheochromocytoma, or medications.

## Risk factors

Risk factors for primary hypertension are broadly divided into non-modifiable and modifiable. Non-modifiable factors include increasing age, family history and Black African or South Asian ancestry. Modifiable factors — the ones that public health campaigns target — include high dietary sodium, low potassium intake, obesity, physical inactivity, excess alcohol consumption, chronic psychological stress, and use of tobacco or NSAIDs. Sleep apnoea is increasingly recognised as both a cause and a consequence of hypertension.

## Clinical features

Most patients are entirely asymptomatic and hypertension is detected on routine screening. When blood pressure rises severely and rapidly (hypertensive emergency), symptoms may include headache — particularly occipital and worse in the morning — visual disturbance, chest pain, dyspnoea, or neurological deficits. These features warrant same-day hospital assessment.

On examination, blood pressure should be measured in both arms using a validated device with an appropriately sized cuff. Fundoscopy may reveal hypertensive retinopathy, and cardiac examination may demonstrate a loud aortic component of the second heart sound or a fourth heart sound in long-standing disease.

## Diagnosis

Diagnosis requires elevated readings on at least two separate visits, ideally confirmed by **ambulatory blood pressure monitoring (ABPM)** or home monitoring over 4–7 days. A work-up for end-organ damage and secondary causes typically includes:

- Urinalysis for protein and blood
- Serum creatinine and electrolytes
- Fasting lipid panel and HbA1c
- 12-lead ECG (left ventricular hypertrophy)
- Consider echocardiography, renal ultrasound and renal artery Doppler when indicated

## Management

Treatment combines lifestyle modification with pharmacotherapy. **Lifestyle measures** include sodium restriction (aim < 2 g/day), weight loss, the DASH-style diet rich in fruit and vegetables, regular aerobic exercise, moderation of alcohol, and smoking cessation. These alone can reduce blood pressure by 8–14 mmHg.

When drug therapy is needed, first-line agents are:

1. **ACE inhibitors** (e.g. ramipril) or **ARBs** (e.g. losartan) — particularly in diabetes or chronic kidney disease
2. **Calcium-channel blockers** (e.g. amlodipine) — preferred in older patients and Black African ancestry
3. **Thiazide-like diuretics** (e.g. chlorthalidone)

Most patients require two or more agents from different classes to reach target. Combination therapy with an ACE inhibitor plus a calcium-channel blocker is a common and effective pairing.

## Prognosis

Hypertension is generally lifelong but very treatable. A 10 mmHg reduction in systolic blood pressure reduces the risk of major cardiovascular events by approximately 20% and stroke by approximately 27%. Untreated, it accelerates atherosclerosis and leads to heart failure, [arrhythmia](/articles/cardiology/arrhythmia) such as atrial fibrillation, chronic kidney disease, retinopathy and dementia.
