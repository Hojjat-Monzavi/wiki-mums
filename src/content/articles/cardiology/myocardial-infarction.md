## Overview

A **myocardial infarction (MI)**, commonly known as a heart attack, occurs when blood flow to a portion of the heart muscle is interrupted for long enough to cause myocardial cell death. The vast majority are caused by acute thrombotic occlusion of a coronary artery at the site of a ruptured atherosclerotic plaque. MI is a medical emergency and remains one of the leading causes of death worldwide.

The classical presentation is crushing central chest pain radiating to the left arm or jaw, but presentations vary — particularly in women, older adults and people with diabetes, who may have minimal or atypical symptoms such as dyspnoea, nausea, fatigue or syncope.

## Classification

MIs are broadly classified by ECG appearance:

- **STEMI** — ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction, indicating full-thickness (transmural) ischaemia and complete coronary occlusion. Requires immediate reperfusion.
- **NSTEMI** — non-ST-elevation MI, partial-thickness ischaemia with raised cardiac biomarkers but no ST elevation.
- **Unstable angina** — ischaemia at rest without biomarker rise.

Together, NSTEMI and unstable angina are termed **non-ST-elevation acute coronary syndromes (NSTE-ACS)**.

## Pathophysiology

The coronary arteries supply the myocardium with oxygenated blood. In atherosclerosis, cholesterol-laden plaques build up in the vessel wall over decades. Most MIs occur when an unstable plaque ruptures, exposing its thrombogenic contents to the bloodstream. Platelets aggregate, the coagulation cascade is activated, and a thrombus forms that partially or completely occludes the lumen. Downstream myocardium becomes ischaemic within seconds; cell death begins within roughly 20 minutes and progresses transmurally over 6 hours if flow is not restored.

Major modifiable risk factors overlap heavily with those for [hypertension](/articles/cardiology/hypertension): smoking, diabetes, dyslipidaemia, family history, obesity and physical inactivity.

## Clinical features

The classic symptom is **retrosternal chest pain** described as pressure, crushing or tightness, lasting more than 20 minutes, often radiating to the left arm, neck or jaw. Associated features include sweating (diaphoresis), nausea, vomiting, dyspnoea, and a sense of impending doom. Pain may be relieved only partially by nitrates.

Atypical presentations are common and easily missed: epigastric pain mistaken for indigestion, isolated dyspnoea in older patients, or silent infarcts in people with diabetes. A high index of suspicion is essential.

## Diagnosis

Diagnosis combines three elements:

1. **History** of ischaemic-type chest pain
2. **ECG changes** — ST elevation, new left bundle branch block, ST depression or T-wave inversion
3. **Elevated cardiac biomarkers** — high-sensitivity troponin, sampled at presentation and again 1–3 hours later

 bedside echocardiography can identify regional wall motion abnormalities but is not required for the diagnosis.

## Management

Immediate management — **MONA** — consists of:

- **M**orphine for refractory pain (use judiciously)
- **O**xygen only if hypoxic (SpO₂ < 90%)
- **N**itrates for ongoing chest pain
- **A**spirin 300 mg chewed, plus a second antiplatelet (ticagrelor or prasugrel)

For **STEMI**, the priority is **reperfusion**: primary percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) within 120 minutes of first medical contact if available, otherwise fibrinolysis. For **NSTEMI**, an early invasive strategy (coronary angiography within 24–72 hours) is preferred in high-risk patients.

Long-term secondary prevention — the "four pillars" — includes dual antiplatelet therapy, a high-intensity statin, an ACE inhibitor or ARB, and a beta-blocker. Cardiac rehabilitation, smoking cessation, diet and exercise substantially reduce recurrence.

## Complications

Complications may occur within hours, days or weeks:

- **Arrhythmia** — ventricular fibrillation is the leading cause of sudden death in the first hour; atrial fibrillation is also common
- **Heart failure** and cardiogenic shock if a large area of myocardium is lost
- **Mechanical complications** — papillary muscle rupture, ventricular septal rupture, free wall rupture (typically days 3–5)
- **Pericarditis** — early (epistenocardiac) or late (Dressler syndrome)

## Prognosis

In-hospital mortality for STEMI has fallen below 5% in well-resourced settings with timely PCI, but remains significantly higher in patients who present late or develop cardiogenic shock. Long-term survival depends on residual left ventricular function, completeness of revascularisation, and adherence to secondary prevention.
