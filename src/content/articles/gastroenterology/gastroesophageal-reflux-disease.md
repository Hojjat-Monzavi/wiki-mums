## Overview

**Gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD)** is a condition in which stomach contents reflux abnormally into the oesophagus, producing symptoms (typically heartburn and regurgitation) or mucosal damage. It is extremely common: roughly 20% of adults in Western countries experience symptoms at least weekly. The vast majority of cases are managed in primary care, but refractory or complicated disease requires specialist input.

GERD is best thought of as a spectrum from occasional physiological reflux through symptomatic reflux without mucosal damage (non-erosive reflux disease, NERD) to erosive oesophagitis, stricture, Barrett's oesophagus, and rarely adenocarcinoma.

## Pathophysiology

The main barrier to reflux is the **lower oesophageal sphincter (LES)**, a region of elevated tone at the gastro-oesophageal junction. Reflux occurs when:

- **LES pressure is low** — scleroderma, pregnancy, smoking, certain foods
- **Transient LES relaxations** occur — the dominant mechanism in most GERD
- **Anatomical disruption** — hiatus hernia, obesity
- **Oesophageal clearance is impaired** — reduced saliva (e.g. Sjögren), impaired peristalsis

Risk factors include obesity, pregnancy, smoking, alcohol, hiatal hernia, and medications that lower LES pressure (calcium-channel blockers, nitrates, anticholinergics, hormones). Diet — fatty foods, chocolate, caffeine, spicy food, citrus — can trigger symptoms but is rarely the sole cause.

## Clinical features

The classic symptoms are:

- **Heartburn** — retrosternal burning, typically postprandial and on lying or bending
- **Regurgitation** — perception of refluxed material reaching the mouth

Atypical or **extra-oesophageal** symptoms include chronic cough, hoarse voice, wheeze, sore throat, dental erosion, and globus sensation. These are easily missed and often treated as separate problems before the underlying GERD is recognised.

**Alarm features** warranting urgent endoscopy:

- Dysphagia
- Odynophagia
- Weight loss
- Anaemia, vomiting, haematemesis
- New onset after age 55

## Diagnosis

In the absence of alarm features, GERD is a **clinical diagnosis**. A **proton-pump inhibitor (PPI) trial** — for example, omeprazole 20 mg daily for 4 weeks — both confirms the diagnosis and provides symptom relief. Investigations are reserved for atypical presentation, alarm features, or refractory symptoms:

- **Upper GI endoscopy (OGD)** — visualises mucosal damage (Los Angeles grade A–D oesophagitis), hiatus hernia, and screens for Barrett's oesophagus
- **24-hour oesophageal pH monitoring** — for refractory symptoms or pre-operative assessment
- **Oesophageal manometry** — to exclude achalasia before anti-reflux surgery

## Management

### Lifestyle
- Weight loss in obese patients (often the most effective intervention)
- Elevate head of bed; avoid lying within 3 hours of food
- Avoid trigger foods and alcohol
- Stop smoking

### Pharmacotherapy
- **Proton-pump inhibitors (PPIs)** — omeprazole, esomeprazole, pantoprazole — first-line for typical GERD; full effect within 1–2 weeks
- **H₂-receptor antagonists** — ranitidine (withdrawn in many markets), famotidine — useful adjuncts, particularly nocturnal breakthrough
- **Antacids / alginates** — for occasional symptom relief; rapid but short-acting

Long-term PPI therapy is generally safe but is associated with modestly increased risks of Clostridioides difficile infection, community-acquired pneumonia, osteoporotic fracture, and hypomagnesaemia. The lowest effective dose should be used.

### Surgical and endoscopic
- **Laparoscopic fundoplication** — for patients with objective evidence of reflux who are PPI-intolerant or wish to discontinue medication
- **Transoral incisionless fundoplication (TIF)** — less invasive alternative in selected patients
- **LINX magnetic sphincter augmentation** — increasingly used

## Complications

- **Erosive oesophagitis** — mucosal breaks visible at endoscopy
- **Oesophageal stricture** — presents with progressive dysphagia for solids
- **Barrett's oesophagus** — intestinal metaplasia of the distal oesophagus; premalignant for oesophageal adenocarcinoma; surveillance endoscopy recommended for confirmed intestinal metaplasia ≥ 1 cm
- **Oesophageal adenocarcinoma** — rare but rising incidence; risk highest in older obese white males with long-standing reflux

## Prognosis

GERD is typically a chronic relapsing condition. Symptoms recur in most patients when PPI therapy is stopped, and many require long-term maintenance therapy at the lowest effective dose. With appropriate therapy the prognosis is excellent; serious complications such as stricture and adenocarcinoma are uncommon, and surveillance of Barrett's oesophagus allows early detection of neoplasia when it does develop.
