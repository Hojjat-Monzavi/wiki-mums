## Overview

**Peptic ulcer disease (PUD)** refers to breaks in the mucosa of the stomach (gastric ulcer) or proximal duodenum (duodenal ulcer) that extend through the muscularis mucosae. It is one of the commonest upper gastrointestinal disorders, with a lifetime prevalence of 5–10%. The two dominant causes are **Helicobacter pylori** infection and **non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs)**, which together account for over 90% of cases.

Although ulcer-related mortality has fallen substantially with eradication therapy and proton-pump inhibitors (PPIs), complications — particularly bleeding and perforation — remain an important cause of emergency hospital admission and death, especially in older patients.

## Pathophysiology

Gastric and duodenal mucosa are protected from acid and pepsin by a **mucus-bicarbonate barrier**, surface phospholipids, and rapid epithelial cell turnover. Peptic ulcers develop when aggressive factors overwhelm this defence.

### Helicobacter pylori
A spiral, urease-producing Gram-negative bacterium that colonises the gastric mucus layer. It causes:

- Chronic antral gastritis with hypergastrinaemia → increased acid → duodenal ulcers
- Multifocal atrophic gastritis with reduced acid → gastric ulcers and increased gastric cancer risk

Transmission is person-to-person, usually in childhood, and prevalence is closely linked to socioeconomic conditions.

### NSAIDs
Systemic inhibition of cyclo-oxygenase-1 (COX-1) reduces prostaglandin production, depleting mucus and bicarbonate secretion and reducing mucosal blood flow. Risk of NSAID ulcer is dose-dependent and increased by age > 65, previous PUD, concomitant antiplatelet/anticoagulant use, and H. pylori co-infection.

### Other causes
- **Stress-related mucosal disease** — in critically ill patients
- **Zollinger-Ellison syndrome** — gastrinoma causing acid hypersecretion
- **Corticosteroids** alone are weak risk factors but potentiate NSAID injury
- **Smoking, alcohol** — slow healing and increase recurrence

## Clinical features

The classic symptom is **epigastric pain** with a characteristic relationship to meals:

- **Duodenal ulcer** — pain 2–5 hours after meals and at night, relieved by food and antacids
- **Gastric ulcer** — pain shortly after meals, sometimes worsened by food, with weight loss

However, many patients have vague dyspepsia, and 20–30% of NSAID-induced ulcers present for the first time with a complication — bleeding or perforation — without antecedent pain.

**Alarm features** warranting urgent endoscopy include haematemesis or melaena, dysphagia, persistent vomiting, weight loss, palpable mass, and new-onset dyspepsia after age 55.

## Diagnosis

- **Upper gastrointestinal endoscopy (OGD)** — the gold standard; allows direct visualisation, biopsy of gastric ulcers to exclude malignancy, and testing for H. pylori
- **Non-invasive H. pylori testing** — urea breath test, stool antigen, or serology — useful in younger patients without alarm features, and for confirmation of eradication
- **Standard 12-lead ECG** — to exclude cardiac ischaemia, which can mimic ulcer pain

In patients under 55 with uncomplicated dyspepsia, a **test-and-treat** strategy for H. pylori is recommended before endoscopy.

## Management

### Eradication of H. pylori
First-line therapy is a 14-day **quadruple** regimen:

- **PPI** (e.g. omeprazole 20 mg BD)
- **Bismuth** subsalicylate
- **Tetracycline**
- **Metronidazole**

or **triple therapy** with PPI + amoxicillin + clarithromycin where clarithromycin resistance is < 15%. Confirmation of eradication by urea breath test is recommended at least 4 weeks after completion.

### Acid suppression
- **PPI** — for 4–8 weeks for NSAID-related ulcers; continue if NSAID must be continued
- **H₂-receptor antagonists** — alternative if PPI not tolerated

### NSAID risk reduction
- Stop NSAID if possible
- Switch to **COX-2 selective inhibitor** (celecoxib) plus PPI for high-risk patients who must continue
- Test and treat H. pylori before starting long-term NSAID therapy in high-risk patients

### Lifestyle
Smoking cessation and alcohol moderation speed healing and reduce recurrence.

## Complications

### Haemorrhage
The commonest complication. Severity ranges from occult bleeding with iron-deficiency anaemia to massive haematemesis and melaena. Management follows the standard GI bleed pathway: resuscitation, intravenous PPI infusion, urgent OGD with endoscopic haemostasis (clips, adrenaline injection, thermal coagulation), and transfusion as needed.

### Perforation
Sudden severe generalized abdominal pain, free intraperitoneal gas on erect chest X-ray or CT, and peritonism. Requires urgent surgical closure, usually laparoscopically.

### Gastric outlet obstruction
From oedema or scarring of the pylorus; presents with vomiting of undigested food and a succussion splash. Endoscopic balloon dilatation or surgery may be needed.

## Prognosis

The vast majority of peptic ulcers heal with appropriate eradication therapy and acid suppression. Recurrence after successful H. pylori eradication is < 10% per year, compared with 60% without eradication. NSAID-related ulcers recur if NSAIDs continue. Mortality is concentrated in older patients presenting with bleeding or perforation, particularly those with significant comorbidity.
