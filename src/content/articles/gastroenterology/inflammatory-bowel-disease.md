## Overview

**Inflammatory bowel disease (IBD)** encompasses two chronic, relapsing inflammatory disorders of the gastrointestinal tract: **Crohn's disease** and **ulcerative colitis (UC)**. Both produce diarrhoea, abdominal pain and weight loss; they share many extraintestinal features but differ fundamentally in their distribution, depth of inflammation, and complications. IBD is distinct from [irritable bowel syndrome](/articles/gastroenterology/irritable-bowel-syndrome), which is a functional disorder without inflammation.

IBD typically presents in adolescence or early adulthood, with a second smaller peak in late middle age. Incidence is highest in developed countries, but rates are rising rapidly in newly industrialised nations, suggesting an environmental contribution. About 1 in 250 people in Western populations live with IBD.

## Pathophysiology

IBD results from a **dysregulated immune response to intestinal microbiota in a genetically susceptible host**. The two main subtypes differ in their pattern:

### Crohn's disease
- May affect any part of the GI tract from mouth to anus (most commonly terminal ileum)
- **Transmural** inflammation — full thickness of the bowel wall
- **Skip lesions** — segments of inflammation separated by healthy bowel
- Granulomas on histology in ~ 30%
- May produce strictures, fistulae, and perianal disease

### Ulcerative colitis
- Confined to the **colon and rectum**
- **Mucosal and submucosal** inflammation only
- **Continuous** disease from rectum proximally
- No granulomas
- May produce toxic megacolon and increased colorectal cancer risk

Genetic susceptibility is polygenic; over 200 loci are now identified, many shared with other immune-mediated diseases. Environmental triggers — particularly **westernisation of diet, antibiotics in early life, and reduced microbial diversity** — are increasingly implicated.

## Clinical features

### Crohn's disease
- Chronic or nocturnal diarrhoea (often non-bloody)
- Abdominal pain, particularly right iliac fossa
- Weight loss, fatigue
- Perianal disease — fissures, fistulae, skin tags
- Mouth ulcers

### Ulcerative colitis
- Bloody diarrhoea with mucus
- Lower abdominal cramping, tenesmus
- Urgency of defaecation
- Systemic features in severe attacks — fever, tachycardia

### Extraintestinal manifestations (both)
- **Arthritis** — peripheral large-joint, axial spondyloarthritis
- **Skin** — erythema nodosum, pyoderma gangrenosum
- **Eye** — episcleritis, anterior uveitis
- **Liver** — primary sclerosing cholangitis (especially UC)
- **Renal** — stones (oxalate in Crohn's with terminal ileal disease)

## Diagnosis

Diagnosis combines clinical, endoscopic, histological and radiological findings:

- **Faecal calprotectin** — non-invasive marker of intestinal inflammation; normal excludes IBD with high confidence
- **Bloods** — FBC (anaemia), CRP, ferritin, B12, folate, vitamin D
- **Stool culture and C. difficile** — to exclude infectious colitis
- **Colonoscopy with biopsies** — defines extent and depth of inflammation; granulomas suggest Crohn's
- **MRI enterography** — for small-bowel Crohn's disease; identifies strictures, fistulae, active inflammation
- **Capsule endoscopy** — for suspected small-bowel disease when MRI is negative

## Management

Treatment is **stepwise**, aiming for **mucosal healing** rather than mere symptom control:

### Mild–moderate disease
- **5-aminosalicylates (5-ASAs)** — mesalazine — first-line for mild UC; less effective in Crohn's
- **Budesonide** — topically active oral steroid with low systemic absorption; for ileocaecal Crohn's

### Moderate–severe disease
- **Systemic corticosteroids** — prednisolone for induction; never for maintenance
- **Biologic therapy** — anti-TNF (infliximab, adalimumab), anti-IL-12/23 (ustekinumab), anti-α4β7 integrin (vedolizumab); transformative for moderate–severe disease
- **Small molecules** — JAK inhibitors (tofacitinib, upadacitinib), S1P modulators (ozanimod) — oral alternatives to biologics

### Maintenance
- **Immunomodulators** — azathioprine, 6-mercaptopurine, methotrexate — steroid-sparing
- Biologics continued long-term
- Annual review with assessment of disease control and surveillance

### Surgery
- **UC**: proctocolectomy with ileal pouch-anal anastomosis (IPAA) — curative
- **Crohn's**: surgery is not curative; reserved for strictures, fistulae refractory to medical therapy, or abscess

### Supportive
- Iron replacement for iron-deficiency anaemia (intravenous preferred)
- Bone protection with long-term steroids
- Smoking cessation — dramatically improves Crohn's outcomes (but may worsen UC)
- Vaccinations and screening before biologic therapy

## Complications

- **Acute severe colitis** — medical emergency; IV steroids, then rescue therapy (infliximab or ciclosporin), then colectomy if refractory
- **Toxic megacolon** — predominantly UC; emergency colectomy
- **Strictures** and **fistulae** in Crohn's
- **Colorectal cancer** — risk rises with duration and extent of colitis; surveillance colonoscopy after 8–10 years
- **Osteoporosis** from chronic inflammation and steroid use

## Prognosis

IBD is chronic and relapsing but rarely fatal. With modern biologic therapy targeting mucosal healing, most patients achieve good quality of life. Crohn's disease often requires multiple surgeries over a lifetime, with cumulative bowel loss; UC is cured by colectomy. Life expectancy is now near-normal in well-treated patients, although mortality is elevated in the first year after severe presentation or surgery.
