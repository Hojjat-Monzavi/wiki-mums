## Overview

**Irritable bowel syndrome (IBS)** is the commonest functional gastrointestinal disorder, affecting 5–10% of the global population. It is characterised by **chronic, recurrent abdominal pain** associated with a change in bowel habit, in the absence of any structural or biochemical abnormality to explain the symptoms. IBS is a **diagnosis of exclusion** in clinical practice, but well-validated symptom criteria allow confident diagnosis without extensive testing in most patients.

IBS profoundly affects quality of life and is a leading cause of work absenteeism. It is more common in women (2:1), often begins in early adulthood, and is frequently comorbid with anxiety, depression, somatisation disorder, and chronic fatigue syndrome.

## Pathophysiology

IBS is best understood as a disorder of the **brain–gut axis**, with multiple contributing mechanisms that vary between patients:

- **Visceral hypersensitivity** — heightened perception of normal intestinal stretch and contraction
- **Altered gut motility** — diarrhoea-predominant, constipation-predominant, or mixed
- **Disordered gut–brain signalling** — psychological stress amplifies symptoms; symptoms in turn drive anxiety
- **Intestinal microbiota dysbiosis** — particularly post-infectious IBS
- **Low-grade mucosal inflammation** and altered immune activation
- **Genetic and epigenetic factors**

About 10% of cases follow an acute gastroenteritis (**post-infectious IBS**), with risk increased by female sex, severity of the initial illness, psychological distress at the time of infection, and antibiotic use.

## Classification

The Rome IV criteria classify IBS by predominant stool pattern, using the Bristol Stool Scale:

| Subtype | Description |
| --- | --- |
| IBS-C (constipation-predominant) | > 25% hard/lumpy, < 25% loose/watery |
| IBS-D (diarrhoea-predominant) | > 25% loose/watery, < 25% hard/lumpy |
| IBS-M (mixed) | > 25% hard/lumpy AND > 25% loose/watery |
| IBS-U (unsubtyped) | Insufficient abnormality to meet above |

Subtype may change over time within an individual patient, which has implications for therapy.

## Clinical features

The Rome IV diagnostic criteria require recurrent abdominal pain, on average at least **1 day per week in the last 3 months**, associated with **two or more** of:

1. Related to defaecation
2. Associated with a change in stool frequency
3. Associated with a change in stool form

Symptom onset should be at least 6 months before diagnosis. Supportive features include abnormal stool frequency, abnormal stool form, straining or urgency, passage of mucus, and bloating — the latter is often the most distressing symptom.

Symptoms are typically chronic and relapsing, often exacerbated by stress, food, or menstrual cycle. Nocturnal diarrhoea waking the patient from sleep is **not** consistent with IBS and suggests organic disease.

## Differential diagnosis and red flags

IBS shares features with many organic conditions, and the role of the clinician is to recognise features that **exclude** IBS and demand investigation:

- **New onset after age 50**
- **Unintended weight loss**
- **Nocturnal symptoms**
- **Faecal blood or anaemia**
- **Family history of colorectal cancer, inflammatory bowel disease, or coeliac disease**
- **Recent antibiotic use** (C. difficile)
- **Persistent diarrhoea** waking the patient at night

In the absence of red flags, limited testing — full blood count, CRP, coeliac serology, and thyroid function — is sufficient to support the diagnosis. Stool calprotectin helps exclude inflammatory bowel disease.

## Management

A trusted therapeutic relationship and clear explanation are the cornerstone. Treatment is symptom-directed and stepped:

### First line — lifestyle and diet
- **Low-FODMAP diet** — fermentable oligosaccharides, disaccharides, monosaccharides, and polyols; effective particularly for bloating and diarrhoea
- **Soluble fibre** (psyllium) for IBS-C; avoid insoluble wheat bran which can worsen bloating
- Adequate fluid intake, regular meals, exercise

### Pharmacotherapy
- **Antispasmodics** — hyoscine, peppermint oil — for pain
- **Loperamide** for IBS-D
- **Linaclotide, plecanatide** (guanylate cyclase-C agonists) for IBS-C
- **Rifaximin** — non-absorbable antibiotic for IBS-D
- **Tricyclic antidepressants** (low-dose amitriptyline) — modulate visceral sensitivity; particularly useful for pain-predominant disease

### Psychological therapies
- **Cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT)**
- **Gut-directed hypnotherapy**
- **Mindfulness-based stress reduction**

These are particularly effective when stress and anxiety are prominent drivers and should be offered before escalating polypharmacy.

## Prognosis

IBS is a chronic relapsing condition. Symptoms fluctuate over years, often with periods of complete remission. Approximately 30% of patients improve over 5 years; 20% worsen. Reassurance that there is no increased risk of cancer or inflammatory disease — once properly investigated — is itself therapeutic. With patient-centred, stepped care, most patients achieve acceptable symptom control.
