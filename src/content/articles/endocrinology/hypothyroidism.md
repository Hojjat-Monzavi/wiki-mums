## Overview

**Hypothyroidism** is the clinical state that results from insufficient thyroid hormone action in tissues. It is one of the commonest endocrine disorders, affecting up to 5% of the population, with a strong female predominance (5–10:1). The condition ranges from subclinical — biochemical abnormality with few symptoms — to overt, and in its most severe form (myxoedema coma) it is a life-threatening emergency.

The vast majority of cases are **primary hypothyroidism**, in which the thyroid gland itself fails. The commonest cause in iodine-sufficient regions is **Hashimoto's thyroiditis**, an autoimmune destruction of the gland. Less common causes include prior radioactive iodine therapy, thyroid surgery, medications (amiodarone, lithium, checkpoint inhibitors), and iodine deficiency — historically the leading global cause.

## Pathophysiology

The thyroid gland produces **thyroxine (T₄)** and smaller amounts of **triiodothyronine (T₃)** under stimulation by thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) from the anterior pituitary. T₄ is largely a prohormone; peripheral tissues convert it to the active T₃ by deiodination. Thyroid hormones act on nuclear receptors to regulate metabolism in nearly every tissue.

In primary hypothyroidism, falling T₄ removes negative feedback on the pituitary, and TSH rises — making TSH the most sensitive marker of early primary disease. In secondary (central) hypothyroidism — pituitary or hypothalamic disease — TSH is low or inappropriately normal with low free T₄.

Hashimoto's thyroiditis is characterised by lymphocytic infiltration of the gland, anti-thyroid peroxidase (anti-TPO) antibodies, and gradual fibrotic destruction. A brief **hashitoxicosis** phase can occur as stored hormone is released.

## Clinical features

Hypothyroidism produces a slowing of metabolic processes across many systems:

- **General** — fatigue, cold intolerance, weight gain (modest), slow movements and speech
- **Skin and hair** — dry skin, coarse brittle hair, loss of lateral eyebrows, non-pitting oedema (myxoedema)
- **Cardiovascular** — bradycardia, diastolic [hypertension](/articles/cardiology/hypertension), exertional dyspnoea, pericardial effusion in severe cases
- **Gastrointestinal** — constipation, decreased appetite
- **Neuromuscular** — muscle aches, weakness, delayed relaxation of deep tendon reflexes, carpal tunnel syndrome
- **Reproductive** — menorrhagia, infertility, galactorrhoea (from TRH-induced prolactin release)
- **Psychiatric** — depression, cognitive slowing ("myxoedema madness" in severe cases)

In children, congenital hypothyroidism causes severe developmental delay unless treated within the first weeks of life — the rationale for universal newborn screening.

## Diagnosis

Thyroid function tests establish the diagnosis:

| Pattern | TSH | Free T₄ |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Overt primary hypothyroidism | High | Low |
| Subclinical primary hypothyroidism | High (typically > 10 mIU/L) | Normal |
| Central hypothyroidism | Low or normal | Low |

Anti-TPO antibodies support an autoimmune aetiology and are positive in > 90% of Hashimoto's thyroiditis. Thyroid ultrasound is not routinely required but may show a heterogeneously hypoechoic gland.

Other laboratory findings that may accompany severe hypothyroidism include hyponatraemia, hyperlipidaemia, elevated creatine kinase, and anaemia (often macrocytic, sometimes from coexistent B12 deficiency in autoimmune polyglandular syndrome).

## Management

**Levothyroxine (T₄) replacement** is the standard treatment. Starting dose depends on age, weight and cardiac status:

- **Young, otherwise healthy adults** — start at the estimated full replacement dose (1.6 mcg/kg/day)
- **Older adults or those with cardiac disease** — start low (25–50 mcg/day) and titrate slowly
- **Pregnancy** — increase dose by ~ 25–30% on confirmation of pregnancy; tight control is critical for fetal neurodevelopment

TSH is rechecked at 6–8 weeks after each dose change, on an empty stomach. Target TSH is typically 1–2 mIU/L in younger patients, slightly higher in older adults (4–6 mIU/L) to avoid overtreatment and atrial fibrillation risk.

Subclinical hypothyroidism is treated when TSH > 10 mIU/L or when symptomatic with positive antibodies, particularly in younger patients and women planning pregnancy.

## Myxoedema coma

A rare but life-threatening complication of severe, long-standing hypothyroidism, typically precipitated by intercurrent illness, cold exposure or sedatives. Features include hypothermia, altered mental status, hypoventilation, hyponatraemia and hypoglycaemia. Treatment is **intravenous levothyroxine** with **hydrocortisone** (to cover possible coexistent adrenal insufficiency), gentle warming and supportive care in intensive care. Mortality remains 20–60%.

## Prognosis

With appropriate levothyroxine replacement, prognosis is excellent and life expectancy normal. Most patients feel better within 2–3 weeks of starting therapy, with full recovery of metabolic status over 3–6 months. Hashimoto's thyroiditis is usually lifelong, though rarely may remit. Regular monitoring — at least annually — ensures that dose adjustments keep TSH in the target range.
