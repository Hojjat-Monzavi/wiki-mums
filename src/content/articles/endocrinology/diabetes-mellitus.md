## Overview

**Diabetes mellitus** is a group of metabolic disorders united by one feature: **chronic hyperglycaemia**. It is one of the commonest chronic diseases worldwide, affecting roughly 1 in 10 adults, and is a leading cause of blindness, kidney failure, amputation and cardiovascular death. Diabetes is broadly classified into:

- **Type 1** — autoimmune destruction of pancreatic beta cells; absolute insulin deficiency
- **Type 2** — insulin resistance with relative insulin deficiency
- **Gestational diabetes** — diabetes first recognised in pregnancy
- Other specific types — monogenic (MODY), secondary to pancreatic disease or drugs

Type 2 accounts for about 90% of all diabetes and is the focus of this article.

## Pathophysiology

### Type 1 diabetes
An autoimmune process destroys beta cells in genetically susceptible individuals, often triggered by an environmental factor (probably viral). Progressive loss of insulin secretion eventually produces overt hyperglycaemia. Most patients present before age 30, but latent autoimmune diabetes in adults (LADA) is increasingly recognised.

### Type 2 diabetes
Insulin resistance in muscle, liver and adipose tissue initially drives compensatory hyperinsulinaemia. Over time, beta-cell function declines and relative insulin deficiency emerges. Risk factors include obesity (particularly visceral), physical inactivity, family history, ethnicity (South Asian, Black African, Hispanic, Indigenous), age, gestational diabetes, and polycystic ovary syndrome.

Hyperglycaemia causes long-term damage through two pathways: **advanced glycation end-products** (AGEs) cross-linking vessel wall proteins, and **polyol pathway** flux generating osmotic stress in susceptible tissues.

## Clinical features

Type 1 diabetes typically presents with the classic triad:

- **Polyuria** — osmotic diuresis from glycosuria
- **Polydipsia** — dehydration-driven thirst
- **Weight loss** — catabolic state from insulin deficiency

A significant proportion of children and young adults first present in **diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA)** — a medical emergency.

Type 2 diabetes may be asymptomatic for years and detected on routine screening. Symptomatic patients describe polyuria, polydipsia, fatigue, blurred vision, recurrent infections (boils, candidiasis), and slow wound healing. Some present with complications — [myocardial infarction](/articles/cardiology/myocardial-infarction), [stroke](/articles/neurology/stroke), neuropathy, retinopathy — without prior diagnosis.

## Diagnosis

Diagnosis requires one of (confirmed on a second day unless symptomatic with glucose > 11.1 mmol/L):

| Test | Diagnostic threshold |
| --- | --- |
| Fasting plasma glucose | ≥ 7.0 mmol/L (126 mg/dL) |
| 2-hour plasma glucose (75 g OGTT) | ≥ 11.1 mmol/L (200 mg/dL) |
| HbA1c | ≥ 48 mmol/mol (6.5%) |
| Random plasma glucose (with symptoms) | ≥ 11.1 mmol/L (200 mg/dL) |

Prediabetes is defined by HbA1c 39–47 mmol/mol (5.7–6.4%) or impaired glucose tolerance.

## Complications

### Acute
- **Diabetic ketoacidosis** — predominantly type 1; hyperglycaemia, ketosis, acidosis (pH < 7.3, bicarbonate < 15)
- **Hyperosmolar hyperglycaemic state (HHS)** — type 2; marked hyperglycaemia (> 33 mmol/L), severe dehydration, altered mental status, minimal ketosis
- **Hypoglycaemia** — usually iatrogenic; < 3.9 mmol/L

### Chronic
- **Microvascular** — retinopathy (leading cause of adult blindness in working-age adults), nephropathy (leading cause of end-stage renal disease), neuropathy (peripheral sensorimotor, autonomic)
- **Macrovascular** — coronary artery disease, cerebrovascular disease, peripheral arterial disease
- **Foot disease** — combining neuropathy, ischaemia and infection; can lead to amputation

## Management

### Type 1 diabetes
- **Lifelong insulin replacement** — multiple daily injections (basal-bolus regimen) or continuous subcutaneous insulin infusion (insulin pump)
- **Carbohydrate counting** and matched prandial insulin
- **Continuous glucose monitoring** (CGM) is now standard of care
- HbA1c target typically 48–58 mmol/mol (6.5–7.5%), individualised

### Type 2 diabetes
Management is stepwise and individualised:

1. **Lifestyle** — dietary modification, weight loss (aim 5–15%), regular physical activity
2. **Metformin** — first-line unless contraindicated
3. **SGLT2 inhibitors** (empagliflozin, dapagliflozin) — particularly in heart failure, cardiovascular disease, or proteinuric CKD
4. **GLP-1 receptor agonists** (semaglutide, dulaglutide) — for obesity, cardiovascular disease
5. Other agents — sulfonylureas, pioglitazone, DPP-4 inhibitors
6. **Insulin** — when oral/injectable therapy is insufficient

Weight loss of ≥ 10–15% can produce **diabetes remission** in selected type 2 patients, an achievable goal with intensive lifestyle programmes or bariatric surgery.

### Comprehensive care
Annual review includes: HbA1c, lipid panel, renal function, urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio, retinal screening, foot examination, blood pressure (target < 130/80 mmHg), and statin therapy for almost all patients. ACE inhibitor or ARB is preferred for hypertension or proteinuria.

## Prognosis

Diabetes shortens life expectancy by an average of 6 years, primarily through cardiovascular disease. Intensive glycaemic control reduces microvascular complications in both type 1 and type 2 diabetes, while blood pressure and lipid control have greater impact on macrovascular outcomes. With modern therapy — particularly SGLT2 inhibitors and GLP-1 receptor agonists — cardiovascular and renal outcomes have improved substantially in recent years.
